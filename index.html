02 · The Spark

How one article lit the fuse

On his tenth birthday in 2019, Sri Nihal Tammana read a news article about a lithium-ion battery that exploded in California. Most kids his age were busy with their presents. He could not look away from the devastation: the immense fires and the contamination that followed.

He had cared about the environment since he was little, growing up watching videos about climate change and hating to see anyone pollute the planet. This felt different, though. The article was the moment he went from caring about the problem to deciding to act on it.

When he looked into it, he learned that battery pollution is a huge problem that almost nobody knows about, and he decided to be one of the first to do something. A fact can tell you a problem exists. A story is what makes you act on it.

03 · The Problem

The numbers almost nobody knows

15B batteries thrown away worldwide every year
~1% of people know it is even a problem
1% aware · 99% unaware

What a single alkaline battery carries into the ground:

  • Zinc
  • Manganese
  • Nickel
  • Cadmium trace

Each battery only holds tiny amounts of these metals. But tiny amounts times billions of batteries add up fast, and they seep into our soil and water. The damage builds slowly and stays out of sight, so most people never notice. That is the part a story has to fix.

04 · The Struggle

Every founder gets told no

He was ten years old, and a lot of people heard his age before they heard his idea. When he went door to door in July 2019 to talk about recycling used batteries, plenty of them brushed him off.

The big organizations were not much easier. Call2Recycle, one of the largest battery recycling groups in North America, said no the first time he asked. For a kid with a clipboard and a stack of battery-shaped flyers, it would have been an easy place to stop.

He kept going anyway.

The advice that kept him going His dad told him he couldn't let one rejection define the project, and to keep pushing until he found a way through.

“You're way too young to be doing something like a non-profit. Come on, you're 10 years old. You should be in school or playing outside.”

05 · Turning Story into Action

Six pivots from idea to movement

Every time the facts alone fell flat, he found a way to make people actually care.

  1. 1

    Lectures fell flat

    In July 2019 he started going door to door with a short pitch and battery-shaped flyers. People got bored fast, and a lot of the flyers probably went straight in the trash.

  2. 2

    Candy for batteries

    So he switched to battery drives with rewards. Kids who brought in used batteries got candy, and suddenly people started paying attention.

  3. 3

    The “Battery Challenge”

    He turned recycling into a contest between classrooms, with a pizza party for the winning class. A game beat a lecture every single time.

  4. 4

    Call2Recycle says yes

    The group that first said no ended up changing its mind. He kept emailing until the CEO said yes and sent battery bins for free.

  5. 5

    COVID forces a pivot

    When the pandemic closed the schools, he moved the whole thing online and told the story on social media instead.

  6. 6

    The story goes global

    It kept spreading until it reached India, which turned a New Jersey project into a worldwide one.

06 · The Bigger Idea

He isn't counting batteries. He's changing culture.

The collection totals matter, but they were never really the goal. What Sri Nihal wants is for people to teach their friends and family, and to pass the story on the way it was once passed to him. Keeping batteries out of a landfill is good. Changing someone's habit for good is better. And one person who goes on to convince ten more is how this whole thing actually grows.

“Your age does not matter. You could be five years old, but if you have determination in your heart, you can change the world.”

Sri Nihal Tammana

Facts inform. Stories move. Recycle My Battery runs on the second one.

07 · Closing

His advice to a ten-year-old

His advice to anyone starting young is simple. Age does not matter. What matters is passion, perseverance, and a work ethic that outlasts the first no. He was ten when a story changed him, and he has spent every year since proving that a young person with enough determination can change things too.

And if he could go back and tell his own ten-year-old self one thing, it would be this:

“Keep pushing. You are going to do great things.”

Sri Nihal Tammana
